This is the next document before development. It follows the roadmap's **Gate 1 — Validate the Problem**, which calls for interviewing **15–20 people** and focusing on actual past behavior rather than hypothetical interest.

# SideQuest — Research Plan

> Status: Active
> Research stage: Pre-MVP validation
> Target participants: 15–20 people
> Primary audience: College students + young professionals
> Product principle: "I want to do this. Who else wants to join?"

---

# 1. Research Objective

The purpose of this research is to determine whether the problem SideQuest is designed to solve actually occurs frequently enough and strongly enough to justify building the product.

The central problem being tested is:

> People want to do activities but sometimes do not have enough people to do them with.

The research should determine:

1. How frequently this happens.
2. What activities people want to do.
3. Why they cannot currently do them.
4. What they currently do instead.
5. Whether finding additional people is actually the problem.
6. What information they need before joining strangers.
7. What makes them uncomfortable.
8. Whether people are willing to join activities with people they do not already know.
9. Whether people would create activities themselves.
10. Whether the SideQuest concept addresses a real unmet need.

---

# 2. Research Principle

Do not ask:

> "Would you use SideQuest?"

Do not treat statements such as:

> "That sounds like a good idea."

as validation.

Instead, investigate actual behavior.

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you wanted to do something but didn't have enough people."

Past behavior is more useful than hypothetical enthusiasm.

---

# 3. Target Participants

Recruit approximately:

> **15–20 people**

Primary groups:

### College Students

Typical age:

16–24

Examples:

- Undergraduate students
- Postgraduate students
- Students living away from home
- Students who participate in sports/clubs
- Students who regularly make social plans

### Young Professionals

Typical age:

22–30+

Examples:

- Early-career professionals
- People living away from their hometown
- People who recently moved cities
- Professionals looking for activities outside work

---

# 4. Participant Diversity

Avoid interviewing only friends who already understand the product.

Try to include people with different:

- Social circles
- Activity preferences
- Levels of extroversion
- Living situations
- Work/study schedules
- Experience meeting new people
- Familiarity with online communities

Include both people who frequently make plans and people who struggle to find people to join them.

---

# 5. Important Participant Segments

Track whether participants fall into categories such as:

### New-to-City

Recently moved to a city and have limited existing social connections.

### Spontaneous

Often decide:

> "I want to do something today."

### Planner

Usually plans activities days/weeks in advance.

### Activity Host

Frequently organizes activities.

### Activity Joiner

Usually joins plans created by others.

### Socially Connected

Already has a large friend group.

### Limited Availability

Has friends but struggles to find people available at the same time.

These are research segments, not permanent SideQuest user types.

---

# 6. Interview Format

Recommended:

> 20–30 minutes per participant

Prefer:

- One-on-one interviews
- In-person or video call
- Natural conversation
- Open-ended questions

Avoid:

- Group interviews
- Leading questions
- Pitching SideQuest too early
- Trying to convince participants of the idea

---

# 7. Interview Structure

Each interview should follow:

```text
Introduction
    ↓
Recent activity behavior
    ↓
Unfulfilled plans
    ↓
Current alternatives
    ↓
Finding people
    ↓
Trust and safety
    ↓
Hosting behavior
    ↓
Concept test
    ↓
Closing questions
````

---

# 8. Introduction Script

Use a neutral introduction.

Example:

> "I'm researching how people make plans and find people to do activities with. I'm not trying to sell anything. I want to understand what you actually do today."

Do not explain the entire SideQuest concept initially.

---

# 9. Section A — General Activity Behavior

### Question 1

> What do you usually do when you want to do something outside your normal routine?

Follow-up:

> What kinds of activities?

---

### Question 2

> Tell me about the last activity or plan you made with other people.

Follow-up:

* What was it?
* Who did you go with?
* How did you organize it?
* How long did it take?
* What communication tools did you use?

---

### Question 3

> How often do you make plans like this?

---

### Question 4

> What activities do you wish you did more often?

Do not suggest categories initially.

Let the participant answer naturally.

---

# 10. Section B — The Missing-People Problem

This is the most important section.

### Question 5

> Tell me about the last time you wanted to do an activity but didn't have enough people.

Follow-up:

* What did you want to do?
* How many people did you need?
* How many people did you have?
* Who did you ask?
* How did you ask them?
* What happened?

---

### Question 6

> What did you do when you couldn't find enough people?

Possible answers may include:

* Cancelled
* Went alone
* Asked friends
* Asked WhatsApp groups
* Posted on Instagram
* Used Reddit
* Used Discord
* Used another app
* Changed the activity
* Postponed it

Do not provide these options unless necessary.

---

### Question 7

> How often does this happen?

Record approximate frequency.

Example:

```text
Never
Rarely
Monthly
Weekly
Multiple times per week
```

---

# 11. Section C — Current Alternatives

### Question 8

> When you need more people for an activity, where do you look?

---

### Question 9

> Why do you use that method?

---

### Question 10

> What works well about it?

---

### Question 11

> What doesn't work well?

---

### Question 12

> Have you ever posted somewhere specifically asking if people wanted to join an activity?

If yes:

* Where?
* What happened?
* How many people responded?
* Did they actually show up?

---

# 12. Section D — Time and Availability

### Question 13

> Is the problem usually that you don't have enough friends, or that your friends aren't available at the same time?

This distinction is important.

---

### Question 14

> How far in advance do you usually make plans?

Examples:

* Same day
* 1–2 days
* Several days
* One week+
* Depends on activity

---

### Question 15

> Are there activities you would join if you could find people at the right time and place?

Ask for specific examples.

---

# 13. Section E — Meeting New People

### Question 16

> Have you ever done an activity with people you didn't already know?

If yes:

* How did you meet them?
* What was the activity?
* How did you feel beforehand?
* How did it go?

---

### Question 17

> Would you consider joining an activity where you didn't know the other participants?

Do not stop at yes/no.

Follow up:

> What would make you comfortable doing that?

---

### Question 18

> What information would you want to know before joining?

Potential areas to listen for:

* Host identity
* Number of participants
* Ages
* Gender mix
* Location
* Activity details
* Cost
* Skill level
* Previous participation
* Verification
* Reviews/reputation

Do not suggest these unless the participant needs prompting.

---

# 14. Section F — Trust and Safety

### Question 19

> What would make you hesitate to join an activity with strangers?

---

### Question 20

> What would make you feel safer?

---

### Question 21

> Would you want to see information about the host before joining?

If yes:

> What information?

---

### Question 22

> Would you prefer to join immediately or ask the host for approval first?

Ask why.

---

# 15. Section G — Hosting

### Question 23

> Have you ever organized an activity yourself?

---

### Question 24

> What is difficult about organizing it?

Listen for:

* Finding people
* Getting responses
* Managing availability
* Managing cancellations
* Communication
* Capacity
* Location
* Cost
* No-shows

---

### Question 25

> If you created an activity and needed three more people, how would you find them today?

---

### Question 26

> Would you be comfortable having strangers join something you organized?

Follow up:

> Under what conditions?

---

# 16. Section H — Concept Test

Only after understanding the participant's current behavior should SideQuest be explained.

Use a short explanation:

> "I'm exploring a product where someone can post an activity they want to do, specify when and where it is and how many people they need, and other people nearby can join or request to join. The goal isn't to build a social feed or dating app. It's mainly about finding the people needed for a specific activity."

Then ask:

### Question 27

> What is your immediate reaction?

Do not defend the idea.

---

### Question 28

> In what situation would you personally use something like this?

---

### Question 29

> In what situation would you not use it?

---

### Question 30

> What would you need to see before joining?

---

### Question 31

> What would stop you from creating an activity?

---

### Question 32

> What would make this significantly more useful than asking your existing friends?

This is a critical question.

---

# 17. Concept Test — Avoid Leading

Do not ask:

> "Would you love an app that solves this?"

Do not ask:

> "Would you use SideQuest every week?"

Do not ask:

> "Isn't this better than WhatsApp?"

Instead ask:

> "How would this compare with what you currently do?"

---

# 18. Final Questions

### Question 33

> If you could change one thing about how you currently find people for activities, what would it be?

### Question 34

> What have I not asked that is important about this problem?

# 20. Research Assumptions

The research should test the following assumptions.

## A1 — The problem exists

People sometimes want to do activities but lack enough participants.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A2 — The problem happens frequently enough

The problem occurs often enough to justify a dedicated product.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A3 — Finding people is the actual bottleneck

The problem is not primarily:

* Cost
* Transportation
* Time
* Lack of interest
* Lack of suitable venues

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A4 — People will join strangers

Users are willing to participate with people they do not already know if sufficient trust/safety information exists.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A5 — People will create activities

Users are willing to host activities rather than only consume existing ones.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A6 — Existing alternatives are insufficient

Current methods such as:

* WhatsApp
* Instagram
* Reddit
* Discord
* Friends
* Existing activity platforms

do not completely solve the missing-participant problem.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A7 — Activity-first is preferable

Users primarily care about:

> "What do I want to do?"

rather than:

> "Which people should I meet?"

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A8 — Capacity is useful

Explicitly showing:

```text
7/10 joined
Need 3
```

helps users understand whether their participation is needed.

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

## A9 — Host control matters

Some activities require:

> Request → Host approval

while others can support:

> Instant Join

Status:

> HYPOTHESIS

---

# 21. Research Evidence Rules

A hypothesis should not be marked validated because:

* One person liked it.
* A friend said it was useful.
* Someone said they "might use it."
* Someone liked the UI.
* Someone said it sounds interesting.

Strong evidence should come from:

* Repeated real experiences
* Recent examples
* Existing workarounds
* Repeated frustrations
* Actual attempts to solve the problem
* Willingness to change existing behavior

---

# 22. Evidence Strength

Use:

### Strong

Repeated behavior across multiple participants.

### Medium

Repeated stated problem with some behavioral evidence.

### Weak

Hypothetical interest or isolated comments.

### Invalid

Researcher interpretation without participant evidence.

---

After all interviews, identify:

### Existing Alternatives

### Trust Barriers

### Host Barriers

### Segment Differences

Do college students and young professionals behave differently?

# 26. Decision Criteria After Research

After interviews, every major assumption should become one of:

```text
VALIDATED
INVALIDATED
PARTIALLY VALIDATED
UNCERTAIN
```

If a critical assumption is invalidated, revisit the relevant product decision before development.

---

# 27. Research → Product Feedback Loop

The research should be allowed to change:

* MVP scope
* Categories
* Discovery
* Join flow
* Host controls
* Safety requirements
* Activity information
* Target audience
* Launch city
* Positioning

The purpose of research is not to prove SideQuest is correct.

The purpose is to determine what needs to change.

---

# 28. Research Completion Criteria

Gate 1 is complete when:

* 15–20 relevant people have been interviewed.
* Interviews focused primarily on actual past behavior.
* Current alternatives have been documented.
* Repeated problems have been identified.
* Major objections have been documented.
* Trust/safety concerns have been documented.
* Hosting behavior has been investigated.
* Major assumptions have been classified.
* Product implications have been documented.
* `RESEARCH-REPORT.md` has been completed.

---

# 29. Important Rule

Do not begin full MVP implementation simply because the product idea sounds good.

First establish:

> **Is this a real problem for enough people, and what exactly is the problem?**

Then build the smallest product that addresses the validated version of that problem.

---

# 30. Research Principle

The objective is not:

> "Convince people to use SideQuest."

The objective is:

> **"Understand how people currently make activities happen, where that process breaks, and whether SideQuest can meaningfully improve it."**
